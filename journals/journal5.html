Journal 5

Mimi Onuoha's The Point of Collection, and what happens to our data after we hand it over

Where it starts

This week's reading was Mimi Onuoha's The Point of Collection. Her main argument is that the problems with data do not start when someone analyses it, but much earlier, at the moment it is collected. Who collects it, how and why all shape what a data set ends up saying. She offers five short ideas about this, and while the essay is fairly brief, it made me think about my own data in a more concrete way. Every sign-up form, app permission and accepted cookie is a small point of collection, and I almost never stop to think about what happens after.

Data that outlives its purpose

One of Onuoha's points is that data sets outlive the reasons they were collected for. A company might ask for my location or contacts for one feature, but once that data exists, it stays, and it can be used for something else entirely. This is where data breaches and leaks come in. When a company is hacked, or data is exposed by accident, information I gave for one simple reason, an email for a receipt or a phone number for an account, ends up somewhere I never agreed to. The original purpose no longer matters. The data is simply out there. It makes me think that the safest data is the data that was never collected in the first place, and that companies rarely ask whether they actually need what they are taking.

Each piece of information seems harmless on its own. It is the combination that makes it feel personal.

Ads that know too much

Onuoha also writes about how combining data sets can reveal more than any single one was meant to. I see this most clearly in personalized ads. I search for something once, mention it near my phone, or linger on a post a little too long, and suddenly it follows me across every app. Each piece of information seems harmless on its own. It is the combination that makes it feel personal. What bothers me is not just that the ads are accurate, but that I never really chose to share the conclusions being drawn about me: my habits, my interests, sometimes even my mood. I gave small pieces, and someone else put the picture together.

The discount that isn't

This connects to something smaller but more everyday: fake discounts. A countdown timer that resets, a "was" price that was never real, an "only 2 left" message, or a deal that seems to appear right after I looked at something twice. Some of these may be pure marketing tricks, but many rely on what a site already knows about me and how I tend to behave. Onuoha describes collection as a relationship between the people collecting and the people being collected, with power sitting on one side. Fake discounts make that imbalance easy to see. The store knows when I am hesitating, and it uses that. I only see the price. Reading this essay did not make me want to stop using technology, but it did make me more aware that every interaction is a small exchange, and that I am usually the one giving more than I realise.
